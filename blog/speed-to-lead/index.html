Speed to lead: why the first 5 minutes decide the sale

Speed to lead is the time between someone enquiring and hearing back from you. For most service businesses it quietly decides who wins the customer: the first business to reply with something useful usually gets the booking. Here's why, what slows teams down, and how to reply in seconds without living on your phone.

By Aftab Ahmed · October 7, 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways
  • Speed to lead is the time from a new enquiry to your first real reply; shorter almost always means more booked customers.
  • Many people contact two or three businesses at once and go with whoever responds first and makes the next step easy.
  • The usual blockers are being busy with customers, after-hours enquiries and leads spread across too many inboxes.
  • An automated first reply that asks one useful question lets you respond in seconds and keep the conversation for yourself.

If you take one thing from this guide: the best follow-up message in the world loses to an average message sent first. Speed to lead is less about clever copy and more about being the business that answered while the customer was still holding their phone.

The good news is that this is one of the easiest problems in marketing to fix. You don't need a bigger team, just a system that replies the moment a lead arrives and hands the conversation to you.

What is speed to lead?

Speed to lead is the time between a lead contacting you (a call, a web form, an ad lead form, a DM) and your first meaningful reply. "Meaningful" matters: an auto-email that says "we got your message" doesn't count if it gives them nothing to respond to.

It applies to every channel. A missed call that gets a text back in ten seconds has a fast speed to lead. A website form that someone answers the next morning has a slow one, even if the reply is excellent.

Why do the first few minutes matter so much?

Because interest fades fast and competitors are one tap away. When someone searches for a dentist, a plumber or an agent, they often fill in two or three forms or call several numbers in a row. The first business that responds with something useful starts the conversation, and the others are often too late to matter.

Research backs up what most business owners feel. A widely cited Harvard Business Review study (2011) found that companies replying to online leads within an hour were far more likely to have a meaningful conversation with the decision maker than those who waited even a little longer, and most companies took much longer than an hour. Fifteen years later, customers expect even faster replies, not slower.

There's a simple human reason too: a quick reply feels like good service. Someone who hears back in minutes assumes you're organized and responsive, which is exactly what they want from the business they're about to hire.

Every lead you pay for through ads has a shelf life. Slow follow-up doesn't just lose customers; it quietly raises your real cost per customer.

What slows small businesses down?

Almost never laziness. Usually it's one of these:

  • You're with a customer. The dentist is treating a patient, the contractor is on a roof, the agent is at a showing.
  • Leads arrive after hours. Evenings and weekends are when many people finally get round to looking for help.
  • Leads are scattered. Web forms in one inbox, Facebook leads in another, portal leads by email, DMs on Instagram.
  • Nobody owns it. Everyone assumes someone else replied.

Notice that none of these are fixed by "trying harder." They're fixed by a system that doesn't depend on someone being free.

How fast are you right now?

Find out before changing anything. For the next two weeks, note for each new lead when it arrived and when it got its first real reply. You can do this in a spreadsheet, or most CRMs will report it automatically.

Look at two numbers: your average response time, and how many leads waited more than an hour. The second number is usually the more painful one, and the more useful to track.

A useful exercise: fill in your own website form at 8pm on a Saturday and see what happens. Then do the same with a competitor's. It's often eye-opening.

How to reply to every lead in seconds

Automate the first reply and route every lead into one place. Here's the setup I'd put in place for most service businesses:

  1. One inbox for every source. Calls, forms, ad leads and DMs flow into a single CRM so nothing is missed.
  2. Missed-call text back. Any unanswered call gets a friendly text within seconds.
  3. Instant first reply to forms and ad leads. A personal text and email go out immediately, using their name and what they asked about.
  4. An AI assistant for the next questions. It answers common questions, asks your qualifying questions and offers real times from your calendar.
  5. Instant alerts for hot leads. When a lead is ready, you or your team get notified with the full conversation.
  6. Follow-up for the quiet ones. Leads who don't reply get a few light, useful follow-ups over the next days.

Here's a quick checklist to see where you stand:

  • Every new lead gets a reply within 5 minutes, day or night
  • Missed calls get an automatic text back
  • All lead sources land in one place
  • Someone is alerted when a lead is ready to book
  • You can see your average response time each month

This is the core of the lead generation and follow-up systems I build, often with an AI assistant that answers and books around the clock. If you run paid ads, it's also the fix for ads that get clicks but no calls.

What should the first message say?

Short, personal and ending with one easy question. For example:

Missed call: "Hi, it's Rapid Air HVAC. Sorry we missed your call, we're out on jobs. What's going on with your system?" (More on this in what missed calls cost HVAC companies.)

Web form: "Hi Sarah, thanks for reaching out about a kitchen remodel. Are you thinking of starting in the next few months, or still planning?"

Avoid long introductions, links to your whole website or a wall of text. The only job of the first message is to get a reply. For more examples you can adapt, see these follow-up text and email scripts.

FAQ

What is a good speed to lead?

Under five minutes is a strong target for most service businesses, and an instant automated first reply is better still. What matters most is that the lead hears from you while they are still looking, before they contact the next business on their list.

Does replying instantly with automation feel impersonal?

Not if the message is short, specific and useful. A text that uses their name, mentions what they asked about and asks one easy question feels attentive. A generic "we received your enquiry" email is what feels impersonal.

How do I measure my speed to lead?

Compare the time each lead came in with the time of your first reply, for a few weeks. Most CRMs can report this automatically. Track the average, but also how many leads waited more than an hour, because those are usually the ones you lose.
