Facebook ads for real estate agents that generate real buyer leads

Can Facebook ads bring real buyer leads for a real estate agent? Yes, but not the way most agents run them. Housing ads have limited targeting, so you can't just aim at "people ready to buy." The offer, the lead form and how fast you follow up do the filtering instead. Get those three right and Facebook becomes a steady source of buyers who actually answer the phone.

By Aftab Ahmed · November 2, 2026 · 9 min read

Key takeaways
  • Real estate ads fall under Meta's Housing special ad category, which removes age, gender and ZIP code targeting and limits detailed targeting.
  • Because targeting is limited, your offer and ad copy decide who responds. Specific offers attract serious buyers; vague "free home search" offers attract browsers.
  • Lead forms with a few qualifying questions and a "higher intent" review step usually produce fewer but better leads than one-tap forms.
  • Most of the value is lost after the click: reply within minutes by text and call, then keep following up for weeks.
  • Judge campaigns by booked appointments and showings, not cost per lead.

When agents tell me Facebook leads are "all junk," the ads are rarely the only problem. Usually it is a mix of a vague offer, a lead form that asks nothing, and a follow-up that starts the next day. Fix those and the same budget tends to produce far more conversations.

This guide covers what I set up for agents: working within the housing rules, offers that filter for intent, form settings, and the follow-up that turns a form fill into a showing.

Why do most real estate Facebook ads bring junk leads?

Because the ad makes it too easy for people with no plans to move. A "See every home in [city]" ad with a one-tap form attracts curious scrollers, renters and people who forgot they filled it in. They are cheap leads, and most never answer.

Three other patterns I see often:

  • No reason to talk to an agent. If the ad only promises listings, people go to the big portals instead.
  • Slow replies. A lead that waits until tomorrow has often forgotten you, or already talked to someone else. The speed to lead guide explains why minutes matter.
  • Measuring the wrong thing. A $4 lead that never answers costs more than a $25 lead who books a showing.

What are the housing ad rules?

Ads for selling or renting homes must run under Meta's Housing special ad category. In practice that means:

  • No targeting by age, gender or ZIP code; locations use a minimum radius around a city or address.
  • Far fewer detailed targeting options, and no exclusions based on them.
  • Lookalike audiences are not available in the usual form.

Meta updates the details from time to time, and its systems can flag housing ads even if you don't select the category, so follow what Ads Manager shows when you set up the campaign. Meta's special ad category documentation lists the current requirements.

The Fair Housing Act applies to your wording too. HUD notes that discriminatory housing advertisements are illegal. Describe the property and the neighborhood's features, not the kind of person who "should" live there. Phrases like "perfect for young professionals" or "great for families" can be a problem; "3 bedrooms, 5 minutes from the light rail" is fine.

Tip: Treat the limits as a reason to write sharper ads. With broad audiences, the ad copy is your targeting. A specific, honest ad finds the right people on its own.

Which offers attract serious buyers?

Offers that only make sense to someone who is actually planning to buy. Some that work well:

  • A specific home or small set of homes, such as a new listing with price, beds and a clear photo, plus "Ask about a showing."
  • A first-time buyer plan for your area: what you need saved, local assistance programs, and the steps from pre-approval to closing.
  • A "what can I afford here" session with you and a local lender, booked for a set time.
  • Off-market or coming-soon alerts, only if you can genuinely deliver them.

Compare that with "Free list of homes in [city]." Anyone can get that from a portal, so it attracts browsers. The more your offer needs a real plan to be useful, the better the leads.

Lead form or landing page?

Both can work. Instant forms are faster to set up and get more leads; landing pages usually bring fewer, more motivated people. For most agents I start with an instant form set up to filter:

  • Choose the higher intent form type, which adds a review screen before submitting.
  • Ask 2 or 3 short questions: timeframe to buy, whether they are pre-approved, and the area or price range.
  • Use a multiple-choice timeframe (0 to 3 months, 3 to 6, 6 to 12, just looking) so you can sort leads right away.
  • Get consent for texts and calls in clear words on the form, with your privacy policy linked.
  • Make the thank-you screen say exactly what happens next ("I'll text you in the next few minutes").

If you want to compare both approaches in detail, a landing page test is worth running once your form campaign is stable.

What should happen after someone fills in the form?

Contact within minutes, by text first and then a call. A simple sequence I build for agents:

  1. Instant text: "Hi Sam, it's Jordan with [Brokerage]. Thanks for asking about 14 Oak St. Are you hoping to see it this week, or just starting to look?"
  2. Call within 5 minutes during working hours. If no answer, leave a short voicemail that mentions the home they asked about.
  3. Day 1 to 3: a text with one useful thing, such as two similar homes or the open house time.
  4. Weeks 2 to 8: a weekly check-in or new listings that match what they said.
  5. Long-term nurture for "6 to 12 months" leads: a monthly market note.

How much should an agent spend?

Enough to get a clear read in 4 to 6 weeks. Many agents start somewhere around $20 to $50 a day for one campaign, but costs vary a lot by market and season. Here is a hypothetical example of how to judge it:

If one in several of those appointments eventually closes, the math can work well even at that cost, though the sale may be months away. A simple campaign plan you can launch this week

  1. Pick one offer: a specific listing or a first-time buyer plan for one area.
  2. Create a Leads campaign, select the Housing special ad category, and set the location to your service area.
  3. Write two ads: one with a listing photo and price, one with a short video of you explaining the offer in 30 seconds.
  4. Build a higher intent form with the three questions above and clear consent wording.
  5. Connect the form to your CRM so every lead gets an instant text and a task to call.
  6. After two weeks, keep the ad with the best cost per conversation, not the cheapest lead.

FAQ

Do I have to use the Housing special ad category for real estate ads?

Yes. Meta requires ads about selling or renting homes to run under the Housing category, which limits targeting by age, gender and ZIP code. If you skip it, Meta may reject the ad or apply the category automatically.

Are Facebook lead forms or landing pages better for real estate?

Lead forms usually get more leads at a lower cost; landing pages usually bring fewer, more motivated people. A lead form with the higher intent setting and 2 or 3 qualifying questions is a good middle ground for most agents.

How fast should I follow up on Facebook real estate leads?

Within minutes if you can. Send an automatic text right away, then call as soon as possible. Leads go cold quickly, and many people contact more than one agent.
