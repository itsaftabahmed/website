Facebook ads for home service businesses (HVAC, plumbing, roofing)

Do Facebook ads work for home service businesses? Yes, for planned jobs: maintenance plans, system replacements, water heater upgrades, roof inspections and remodels. They rarely work for emergencies, because nobody scrolls Instagram while their basement floods. Promote a clear offer for a planned job to homeowners in your service area, collect a few qualifying details, and reply within minutes.

By Aftab Ahmed · November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

Key takeaways
  • Use Facebook ads for planned jobs and Google search for emergencies.
  • The offer matters more than the targeting: a specific, local offer beats a generic discount.
  • Ask 2 or 3 qualifying questions in the form so you are not calling renters or people outside your area.
  • Reply to every lead within minutes by text, then call, because Facebook leads cool off fast.
  • Judge the ads on booked jobs and revenue, not on cost per lead.

A common belief among contractors is that "Facebook ads don't work for my trade." Usually the ads are fine. The problem is that they are promoting the wrong kind of job, with a vague offer, to people who never get a fast reply. Fix those three things and Facebook becomes a steady source of planned work.

Do Facebook ads work for HVAC, plumbing and roofing?

They work well for jobs people plan ahead and poorly for emergencies. Facebook and Instagram show ads to people who are not searching for anything, so the ad has to create the idea. That suits a furnace tune-up before winter or a roof inspection after a hailstorm. It does not suit a burst pipe at 9 pm, when the homeowner goes straight to Google and calls the first company that answers.

So treat Facebook as the channel for demand you create and Google as the channel for demand that already exists. The table below shows which jobs fit where. If you also run search ads, my Google Ads service covers the emergency side.

Which jobs should you advertise on Facebook?

TradeGood for Facebook (planned)Better on Google (urgent)
HVACMaintenance plans, seasonal tune-ups, system replacement, smart thermostats, air qualityNo heat, no cooling, emergency repair
PlumbingWater heater replacement, repiping, water filtration, bathroom upgradesLeaks, clogs, burst pipes, sewer backups
RoofingInspections, full replacement, gutters, skylights, financing offersActive leaks, storm damage happening now

The higher the ticket and the longer the decision, the better Facebook tends to fit, because you get time to follow up and build trust before the homeowner chooses.

What offers work for home service businesses?

A specific offer for a specific job, in a specific area. "Quality HVAC service" gives nobody a reason to act. These kinds of offers give them one:

  • Seasonal tune-up: a set price for a furnace or AC check before the season, with what is included spelled out.
  • Maintenance plan: two visits a year plus priority scheduling. Plans also give you repeat revenue.
  • Free replacement estimate: for water heaters, HVAC systems or roofs, ideally with monthly financing options if you offer them.
  • Inspection with photos: roof or attic inspections where the homeowner gets photos of what you found.

Show your real trucks, your crew and finished work in the creative. Stock photos of smiling models look like every other ad. A 20-second phone video of a technician explaining the offer usually feels more trustworthy than a polished graphic. My guide to why Facebook ads get clicks but no calls covers the offer-and-creative side in more depth.

Tip: Never advertise a discount you can't explain or a "free" service with a hard upsell attached. Homeowners talk to neighbors, and reviews will reflect it.

Instant forms or a landing page?

Both work. The choice depends on how many leads you can handle and how qualified they need to be.

  • Instant forms (the form opens inside Facebook) get more leads because they are prefilled and quick. Use the form setting that adds a review step before submitting, and add 2 or 3 short questions, for example "Do you own the home?", "What do you need?" and "When do you want it done?". That filters out a lot of casual taps.
  • A landing page on your own site usually brings fewer but more serious leads, and it lets you show reviews, photos and financing details. It also gives you better tracking.

Whichever you use, send conversions back to Meta. The Conversions API lets your CRM report which leads actually booked, so the ad system learns to find more people like them instead of more people who only fill in forms.

How should you target homeowners?

Set the location to the areas you actually serve, using a radius around your shop or a list of ZIP codes, and exclude places you don't want to drive to. Beyond that, keep targeting fairly broad and let the offer and the form questions do the filtering. Very narrow interest targeting often makes ads more expensive without making leads better.

Two audiences worth adding when you have them: your past customers (for maintenance plans and referrals) and people who visited your website but didn't book. Upload customer lists only for people who agreed to hear from you.

What should happen after someone fills the form?

A reply within minutes, then a call. Facebook leads are impulse decisions, and the interest fades fast. When a lead comes in during a job, a text should go out automatically:

"Hi Jen, this is Mike from Rapid Air. Thanks for asking about a furnace tune-up. We have Tuesday at 9 am or Wednesday at 2 pm. Which works?"

Then follow up for several days if they don't answer, and stop as soon as they reply. My guide on speed to lead explains why the first minutes matter, and automated lead follow-up shows a full sequence. Automated texts and calls need the person's consent, so the form should say clearly that you'll contact them, and every text should include a way to opt out. The FCC has confirmed that AI-generated voices in robocalls fall under existing calling rules, so any automated calls need the same care.

  • Form asks if they own the home, what they need and when
  • Automatic text within a minute, with two real appointment times
  • Call from a person the same day during business hours
  • Follow-up for about a week if there is no answer
  • Every lead and its outcome tracked in a CRM

How much should you spend, and how do you measure it?

Work back from the jobs you want, not from a round number. A hypothetical example: you want 8 extra replacement estimates a month, 1 in 4 leads books an estimate, and leads cost around $40 in your market. That is 32 leads, or about $1,280 a month. Your real cost per lead will differ by trade, season and city, so treat the first month as a test. My guide to Facebook ads costs for small businesses covers typical ranges.

Measure booked jobs and revenue per dollar spent, not cost per lead. A $25 lead that never answers costs more than an $80 lead that books a $9,000 system replacement. FAQ

Are Facebook ads good for HVAC companies?

Yes, for planned work such as maintenance plans, seasonal tune-ups and system replacements. For emergency repairs, Google search ads usually work better because the homeowner is actively searching for help right now.

Should home service companies use Facebook lead forms or a landing page?

Lead forms bring more leads with less effort; a landing page brings fewer, more serious leads and better tracking. Many contractors start with lead forms that use a review step and 2 or 3 qualifying questions, then add a landing page once volume is steady.

Why are my Facebook leads not answering the phone?

Usually because the reply came too late or the form was too easy to submit by accident. Send an automatic text within a minute with two appointment times, add qualifying questions to the form, and follow up for several days before giving up.
